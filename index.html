How stories for little kids are built

« Il était une fois… »

A storytelling session for CAPE French · Keimon Archibald-Lake · CFBC · [date]

Welcome. Set the ground rule straight away and make it the joke of the session: "I don't speak French. You do. So every French line on these slides belongs to you — I point, you read." That turns your non-French into their job, and they get to be the experts for ninety minutes. You teach the craft in English; they supply the French. Then the tone: "You will not sit still today. You will talk, argue and read out loud." Replace [date] before presenting.

How today works

I don’t speak French — you do. I’ll point at the French; you read it out loud.

When I say “the problem”, you give me the French. That word is the one your exam will want.

Give them a minute to copy this down, or hand it out as a sheet. These are the terms they need to analyse a text in French, and every one of them is a craft idea we will meet today. Set the routine here and keep it all session: you say the English term, then point at the room and THEY give you the French. Never attempt the French yourself — you do not need to, and handing it over is exactly what keeps them working. Repetition works on eighteen-year-olds too, which is itself today's lesson.

Part one

Know your
audience

A three-year-old and a seven-year-old are not the same reader. Write for the wrong one and the book gets closed.

This is the section where most new children's writers go wrong: they write one vague story for "children". Tell them the next three slides are the single most useful thing they will take away today.

Know your audience · 1 of 3

Ages 1–3 · the listening years

Very short

A few words a page. The whole book is read in three minutes, then read again.

Repetition

The same line comes back. The child joins in before they can read.

Bright pictures

Big, bold shapes. The picture carries the meaning.

Familiar objects

Cup. Dog. Shoe. Mummy. Things they can point at in the room.

Example

“Brown Bear, Brown Bear,
What Do You See?”

En français

« Petit Ours Brun » · « T’choupi »

Mêmes ingrédients : phrases très courtes, répétition, images vives, objets familiers.

At this age the adult is the reader and the child is the listener, so you are writing for two people at once: a toddler who wants rhythm and a tired parent who has to read it fourteen times. Ask: "What topics interest a child this age?" Expect answers like food, animals, bedtime, mummy, the dog. Every one of those is a book.

Know your audience · 2 of 3

Ages 4–5 · the “read it again” years

Simple plots

One problem, one solution. No subplots, no flashbacks.

Funny situations

Silly beats clever. A hat on a dog is comedy gold.

Predictable patterns

They want to guess what comes next — and be right.

Counting & naming

Days, colours, foods, numbers. They are learning while you entertain.

Example

“The Very Hungry
Caterpillar”

En français

« La chenille qui fait des trous »

Le même livre ! Et « Le Loup qui voulait changer de couleur » : un schéma répétitif que l’enfant devine.

This is the picture-book sweet spot and where most of them will aim. Point out that the Hungry Caterpillar is doing three jobs at once: telling a story, teaching days of the week, and teaching counting, all inside a pattern the child can predict. Ask: "What topics interest children at this age?"

Know your audience · 3 of 3

Ages 6–8 · the “let me read it myself” years

Adventures

The character leaves home, takes a risk, comes back changed.

Problem solving

Let the child work out the answer before the character does.

Humour

Jokes, wordplay, a character who is wrong in a funny way.

Richer vocabulary

New words are fine here — the sentence around them teaches the meaning.

Example

“The Day the
Crayons Quit”

En français

« Le Petit Nicolas » · « Max et Lili »

Vocabulaire plus riche, humour, et un enfant qui résout son problème tout seul.

The shift here is big: the child is reading alone, so they must not be bored and must not be embarrassed. Mention that a six-year-old can handle a word like "furious" if the sentence shows what it means. Ask: "What topics interest children at this age?" and listen for friendship, fairness, school, being left out.

Mini activity · 4 minutes

In pairs, brainstorm three story ideas a child would love. One sentence each. Then pick your favourite and keep it — you will use it all session.

A missing turtle beats a scientific breakthrough. Every time.

Read both lines out loud with real contrast. Then run the pairs. Walk the room while they work and collect two or three good ones to read back. Tell them to keep the idea they choose, because the character, problem and ending activities later all build on it, and by the end of the session they will have the bones of a real story.

Décrire un personnage

maladroit(e) · curieux / curieuse · gentil(le) · drôle · courageux / courageuse · timide

Ask a child about a book and they will not describe the plot. They will describe the character.

Push on the first card. Many of your students grew up reading books where no character looked like them. Ask who can name a picture-book character from their own childhood who looked Kittitian or Nevisian. The silence is the lesson, and it is also the opportunity.

Time it properly and be strict: sixty seconds forces instinct instead of perfectionism. Then have them read it as one sentence, like "Bo is a slow donkey who wants to win one race." Part three · the craft

Don’t

“The child experienced great disappointment.”

Do

“Emma felt sad.”

Short sentences

One idea per sentence. Full stops are your friend.

Familiar words

The words they hear at home, in the yard, at school.

Natural conversation

Write the way a person actually speaks to a child.

La même règle en français

✗ « L’enfant éprouva une grande déception. »  →  ✓ « Emma était triste. »

Simple is powerful.  La simplicité, c’est la force.

Warn them about the trap: writing for children tempts you to show off your vocabulary to prove the book is "literary". Resist it. The skill is saying something real in words a five-year-old owns. That is harder than writing the long version, not easier.

Fun challenge

5 minutes

Who can translate this for a five-year-old?

The grown-up version

“Despite her considerable trepidation, the diminutive amphibian eventually commenced her aquatic descent.”

One possible answer

“The little frog was scared. But she jumped in anyway. Splash!”

Round two · en français

↳ A student reads the pompous version aloud.

« Malgré son appréhension considérable, le petit amphibien entreprit sa descente aquatique. »

« La petite grenouille avait peur. Mais elle a sauté. Plouf ! »

Best translation wins. Read yours out loud — if it sounds like a textbook, it loses. Si ça sonne comme un manuel, c’est perdu.

Run it as a real competition in pairs; a small prize helps. Read the ENGLISH sentence yourself in a deliberately pompous voice, then hand the French one to a confident student and let them ham it up — it is funnier coming from them anyway. The French round is the same sentence, so they can compare both solutions side by side, which is genuinely useful for CAPE. After you click the answers, point out what the good versions did: they cut the adjectives, they used a sound word, and they kept the feeling. Ask the room which sound word is stronger in French and let them argue it out.

En français · une comptine

↳ A student leads this one.

« Promenons-nous dans les bois…
Loup, y es-tu ? Que fais-tu ?
M’entends-tu ? »

Read the Kittitian example and let the room answer "Nar me" by the third line without being asked — they will. Then hand the French card over: pick a student to lead the comptine and let the rest chant the response. Most of them will know it from primary school, so it runs itself. Stand back and let it happen — you have just proved the point in a language you do not speak, which is the strongest possible demonstration that the repetition is doing the work, not the words. Say exactly that out loud afterwards. Every language builds its children's rhymes this way, and a CAPE candidate who can name "la répétition" in a text has just picked up marks.

The craft

Make the words dance

Rhyme

Only if it is effortless. A forced rhyme is worse than none.

Alliteration

The same sound again and again. Children hear it before they understand it.

Musical language

Sound words: splash, thump, whoosh, crrrack.

Listen to this one

“Sammy snake slid silently through the grass.”

This is the same sound work that teaches children to read. You are writing a phonics lesson that does not feel like one.

Read the Sammy line slowly and let them hear the hiss. Then connect it to what they already know: alliteration and rhyme are phonological awareness in disguise. A writer who uses them well is handing teachers a tool. Warn them hard about forced rhyme — it is the single most common mistake in new children's manuscripts.

Un virelangue français

↳ Who can say it fastest? Time them.

« Trois tortues trottaient sur un trottoir très étroit. »

Winner reads theirs standing up. Bonus point for a French one.

This one always gets loud. Let it. You read the English example; the French virelangue belongs to them — run it as a speed contest and time them on your phone, three attempts each. You are the judge, not a contestant, and the room will enjoy that far more. Collect three or four of their own sentences in either language. Tie it back: when a child hears this, they are practising the sound of a letter without a worksheet in sight, and that is true in English and in French.

Show the feeling in what the character does, not in a label: “Emma’s lip shook” beats “Emma was sad.” — « La lèvre d’Emma tremblait. »

Make the show-don't-tell point gently, because for the youngest readers a plain "Emma felt sad" is actually correct and useful. 3 minutes

I hold up a card. You write a single sentence of a story where a character feels that way — without naming the feeling.

If you get “scared”

If you get “excited”

Run two or three rounds with physical emotion cards. The guessing is the fun part and it proves the point: if the room can name the feeling without being told it, the writing worked. If they cannot, the sentence needs a clearer action.

Story shape

Children want hope at the end

Solve the problem

Actually solve it. Don’t let an adult arrive and fix everything.

Teach naturally

The lesson lives in what happened, not in a speech at the end.

Leave them smiling

The last line is the one they carry to bed.

Avoid

“And so Ben learned that sharing is caring and that friends are important and that…”

Better

“The shy rabbit finally joined the game — and ran home with two new friends.”

Be blunt about preaching: children smell a moral lecture instantly and it ruins the book they just enjoyed. If the story works, the lesson is already in it. Also make the point that the child character should solve it themselves; it is their story, not the adult's.

Energizer

6 minutes

Pass the story

I start:

“One morning, an elephant woke up and discovered polka dots all over its body…”

« Un matin, un éléphant se réveilla et découvrit des pois partout sur son corps… »

↳ Round two: a student reads this, and the lap runs in French.

One sentence each

Going around the room. No passing, no editing, no thinking too long.

It will go sideways

Good. That is imagination doing its job.

Last person lands it

They must solve the polka dots in one sentence.

This is exactly how a child tells you a story. Nothing is off limits.

Keep it fast and do not let anyone rewrite what came before. When it ends, ask what they noticed: the story found a problem on its own, repeated itself, and got funnier. Those were not accidents, they are the instincts the whole session has been training. Nominate the last person early so they can be ready to land it. You start the English lap. For the French lap, hand the opening line to a student and step out of the circle entirely — you will not follow every sentence, and that is completely fine. Watch their faces instead and listen for the laughter, which tells you it is working. Afterwards ask in English what the story did: where the problem appeared, what repeated, how it landed.

Remember this one thing

Children may forget the exact words you write.
But they will remember how the story made them feel.

« Les enfants oublieront peut-être vos mots. Mais ils se souviendront de ce que l’histoire leur a fait ressentir. »

↳ Ask one student to read the French line and close the session.

Read the English yourself, slowly, and let the second half sit in silence for a beat. Then ask one student to read the French line aloud to the room — ending the session in their language rather than yours is the right closing note, and it costs you nothing. Everything in the session — the character, the repeating line, the problem, the ending — exists to produce a feeling the child keeps.
